What a public market benchmark can and can't tell you about whether a deal was priced well

THE SITUATION
A pricing team needs to know if a deal was priced well — and what to trust when checking
There's no public dataset of real negotiated deals with win/loss outcomes, so this project uses a strong analog: LBNL's utility-scale solar PPA database, benchmarked against a third-party market-price index — the same kind of check a pricing desk runs before signing off on a deal.
THE QUESTION
Are recent solar PPA deals priced well relative to the market — and can a public price index actually answer that?
Data & approach
LBNL project data
1,775 real utility-scale solar projects, 2007–2024. Genuinely structured — a clean Excel workbook, not scraped prose.
LevelTen & Trio index
Third-party regional PPA price benchmark, 7 organized markets. Confirmed its actual methodology before trusting it — see the diagnosis ahead.
Method
Filter to disclosed-price PV projects, join to the regional benchmark, compare — then check whether that comparison is actually fair.
1,759 PV projects after excluding CSP. Only 425 (24%) disclose a PPA price at all — a real selection-bias constraint kept in view throughout.

FINDING 2 — A MISTAKE CAUGHT BEFORE IT SHIPPED
The first version of this analysis said 2011 projects were overpriced by +$194/MWh. That number is wrong.
It came from comparing 2011-vintage project prices directly against the 2024 market benchmark — two prices from cost eras 14 years and an 85% industry decline apart. Not a pricing signal. A vintage mismatch.
Wrong approach
Compare ALL vintages (2007–2024) against the single 2024 benchmark
Fixed approach
Restrict the benchmark comparison to matching-vintage (2022–24) projects only

THE DIAGNOSIS — WHY
An asking price and a closing price are not the same measurement.
“The offers underlying our Index are from projects that are currently under development and posted by developers to the LevelTen Marketplace… giving the industry a transparent and unprecedented look at actual PPA price offers — not estimates of what PPA prices could or should be.”
— LevelTen Energy, PPA Price Index methodology
The index is a P25 offer price for projects still seeking a buyer. LBNL's data is a realized, signed price for completed projects. A closing price coming in below an asking-price index is close to the expected outcome — not evidence of negotiation skill.
RECOMMENDATION
Check what a benchmark measures before trusting a comparison built on it.
1. Match the deal stage
Don't compare a signed price against an offer/asking-price index without adjustment. Use a signed-deal comparator, or an internal same-vintage benchmark, if one is genuinely needed.
2. Always vintage-match
This project's own first pass got this wrong (+$194/MWh) before it was caught. Check vintage alignment before any two-price comparison, every time.
3. State the disclosure rate
Only 24% of PV projects disclose a PPA price. Say so up front in any summary — don't let a reader assume full market coverage.
LIMITATIONS & ASSUMPTIONS
  • CSP plants excluded (16 of 1,775 projects) — PPA structure isn't directly comparable to PV
  • 184 of 425 disclosed-price projects (43%) sit in regions with no matching third-party benchmark at all (non-ISO West, non-ISO Southeast, Hawaii)
  • NYISO's own benchmark value is an Excel formula error in LBNL's source workbook — genuinely unavailable, not a small-sample issue
  • Segment sample sizes remain modest even after the vintage fix (n=5 to n=21) — real numbers, not large enough for strong causal claims about why any one segment differs
  • This is a public-data analog for a deal-pricing problem — there's no "lost deal" in this dataset, only completed projects
